# Scenes and the storyboard

A scene is one beat of the composition: one idea, one arrangement, held long enough to
read. Scenes play in the order the storyboard names.

- Plan the storyboard before authoring: the form, the scenes and the order they play in.
- One idea per scene. Do not split one idea across two scenes, and do not put two ideas in
  one scene.
- A declared count names content scenes. The opening title scene, when the brief asks for
  one, is additional to them and sits before them.
- A scene has no fixed length; give each the time its idea needs to read, and keep the
  composition's total to the declared duration.

## The story

The clip makes one argument, not a summary of the article.

- The intro names the subject and the question the clip answers. A title card that only
  names the subject is not an intro.
- Each scene carries one idea. If you cannot state the scene's takeaway in one sentence, or
  it carries two ideas, split it or cut it.
- The middle explains the mechanism or the trade-off in an order a viewer can follow: cause
  before effect, the state before the change, the failure after the design.
- The conclusion answers the intro's question with the content's own outcome. It is not a
  summary and not a generic closer.
- The closing frame carries the conclusion and the Source together; the conclusion does not
  fade before the Source. A Source-only close is for a clip with no conclusion.
- A scene must be understood without the post text. If it needs a word the post carries,
  put that word on screen or split the scene.

## The content is the only source

The composition summarizes the content the brief carries. That content's own claim is the
spine — the outcome, the condition, the figure it states — and the scenes carry it.

- Every claim and every number on screen traces to a sentence in the content. A claim, a
  role, a comparison or a figure the content does not state is a defect, even when it is
  true of the subject.
- Keep the content's qualifications. A claim made with a condition keeps it: never drop
  the condition, invert a role, or promote "only when X" to always.
- A scene may compress the content; it may not add to it.

## A shape is a claim

A shape or an arrow states a relationship. Use only the relationship the content states.

- Stacked = hierarchy. Side by side = peers. An arrow = cause or direction. A loop =
  recurrence. Dashed = eventual or optional. Filled = present; outline = absent. Size =
  importance.
- A shape says what it does: a fork for a DAG, offset bars for a log, stacked strata for a
  warehouse. Never a generic icon collage, an app icon or a logo.
- An icon names the thing it sits on. It is not decoration; an icon the viewer must decode
  is a label, not an ornament. The brand defines no emoji: use a shape, not an emoji.
- One metaphor per scene. Do not mix a stack, a flow and a timeline in one frame.

## Attention

- Every scene has one focal point and one emphasis device that lands on the point: a
  highlight, a pulse, a counter, a build, a camera move. Animate the one thing the viewer
  should look at, not everything.
- The opening gives a reason to stop: the question, the surprising number, the before and
  after, the tension. Do not open on a slow title that only names the subject.
- A scene that is a wall of labels, or whose emphasis is decoration, is a defect. If
  removing the emphasis loses no meaning, it was decoration.
- Order follows the argument: the cause before the effect, the connector after both shapes,
  the number after the thing it measures. Never animate an arrow before the shapes it joins.

## Copy on screen

A clip is watched, not read: a scene is read once, on a phone, and leaves before anyone
finishes a sentence, so the words must land in the time it has.

The person's feedback is a requirement, not advice. A line it asks for stays even where
the budget below says otherwise; so does a line added because the feedback says a scene
does not make its point. Make room by cutting another label, never by dropping either
line. An internal review finding that conflicts with the feedback is resolved without
losing what the feedback asked for.

- Everything visible counts against one budget: one line of six words at most, up to
  three labels of one or two words naming what is shown, and one figure when the idea is
  a number. No eyebrow, subtitle, legend, caption or closing line adds to it. At the ten
  seconds a LinkedIn scene holds, that is what fits; a shorter scene carries less, and a
  scene that needs more is two scenes or copy that gets cut — a smaller font never fits it.
- The budget is a ceiling, not a target. When a scene is over, cut a label before the line
  that states the point. When the shapes alone do not make the idea, one explanatory line
  of eight words or fewer is allowed; prefer cutting a label to cutting that line.
- A poster is one page, not a scene: it may carry the headline, up to three figures, up to
  four short points and the Source on one card. It is still one argument, not a summary;
  every line must earn its place. Animate the page with a few moving lines, shapes or
  light; do not turn it into a sequence of scenes.
- Put the outcome on screen, not the explanation: "3x faster", "one pipeline", "zero
  retries". The sentence behind it belongs in the post text.
- Type is the message. Size the line to be read at phone width in the first second, and
  give the rest of the frame to the subject and its motion.
- A label names a thing that moves or a part that matters; it is never a caption and
  never a second sentence.
- When the brief ends with a `Source:` line, the closing scene prints that line exactly as
  it is given: no rewording and no placeholder. A story clip whose content states an
  outcome carries that outcome and the Source on the closing frame together, separated,
  and neither fades the other out. The Source is alone only when the clip has no
  conclusion to close on. The conclusion is the scene's claim, not a closing line; it is
  the one line the close exists for.
- These govern what plays: a video, a GIF and a deck's rendered video. A slide the reader
  clicks through may carry its supporting points, and an SVG on a page may carry a label.
